One glitch.

01 — The brain's metronome

Your brain runs on rhythms, and speed means something

Neurons don't just fire, they fire in coordinated waves, and different speeds of wave show up during different kinds of mental work. Slow waves, called theta (about 4–8 cycles per second), dominate when the brain is idling — drowsy, unfocused, drifting. Fast waves, called beta and gamma (roughly 13–100 cycles per second), dominate during normal alert processing, when a brain region is actually doing its job. A healthy thalamus and cortex trade off between these speeds constantly, all day, without you ever noticing.

02 — When the metronome gets stuck

Thalamocortical dysrhythmia: one circuit, stuck slow

The thalamus is a relay station: almost everything you see, hear, and feel passes through it on the way to the cortex, and it normally relays that information using fast rhythms. The dysrhythmia theory, first proposed by neuroscientist Rodolfo Llinás and colleagues in the late 1990s, says that in some people a specific thalamic circuit gets stuck firing in slow theta instead — and that this slow rhythm then leaks into whatever cortical area that circuit feeds, dragging normal fast processing down with it. The cortex on the receiving end doesn't stop working; it just starts working wrong.

03 — Why one glitch looks like three diseases

Same problem, different address

What the leaking theta rhythm actually feels like depends entirely on which cortical neighborhood it lands in — the mechanism is identical, only the zip code changes.

01

Auditory cortex → tinnitus

The circuit that should carry sound information runs slow, and the brain reads that distorted, theta-heavy pattern as a phantom tone that isn't actually there.

02

Somatosensory cortex → pain

The circuit that should carry touch and body-position information runs slow instead, and ordinary or absent input gets misread as pain.

03

Prefrontal / limbic → depression

The circuits behind mood regulation and executive function run slow, and the result looks less like a single symptom and more like a persistent low mood.

This is a genuinely useful idea specifically because it's testable: it predicts one measurable EEG signature, not three separate ones, and today it's a respected but still-debated framework, not settled fact. That's exactly what makes it worth checking on a real brain instead of just reading about.

04 — What the headset actually measures

Two numbers, both derived from the same raw wave

A NeuroSky headset streams your raw EEG at 512 samples per second. NeuroDex slices that stream into short windows and runs each one through an FFT (fast Fourier transform) — a standard way of taking a wiggly signal and asking "how much of this is slow, and how much is fast?" That breakdown produces two numbers worth knowing:

θ/γ

The ratio

How much slow theta power is present compared to fast beta and gamma power. High theta relative to gamma is the pattern the dysrhythmia theory predicts; more gamma relative to theta is the normal, fast-dominant pattern. This is what the Focus Index in the app is built from.

SEF95

Spectral edge frequency

The frequency below which 95% of your brain's power sits. Think of it as "where the center of gravity of your brain activity is." A healthy, fast-dominant brain has a higher edge frequency; a theta-heavy one sits lower.

4×/s

Update rate

Both numbers get recomputed about four times a second while you sit with the headset on, which is fast enough to give you feedback in something close to real time, not just a score at the end.

05 — Measured against you, not a chart

There's no "normal" number to hit

The dysrhythmia signature isn't a fixed number that applies to everyone; a resting theta/gamma ratio varies a lot from person to person even with nothing wrong. So the first minute of every session is spent quietly measuring you: your own resting theta, alpha, beta and gamma levels, with no expectation attached. Every score after that is a z-score against that personal baseline — a measure of how far you've moved from your own resting rhythm, not from someone else's. A good session means good for you.

06 — Keeping the signal honest

A blink is not a brainwave

A single dry electrode on your forehead doesn't just pick up cortical rhythm — a single blink swings the signal harder than any real EEG activity does, and a jaw clench can bury the whole spectrum under muscle noise. Left alone, either one would fake the exact pattern this project is trying to measure. Six filters run on every window before it reaches your score, and the same recording can optionally be re-checked offline through MNE-Python, a research-grade EEG toolkit, as an independent second opinion. See exactly how on the Models tab.

07 — How the training loop works

Neurofeedback, delivered as a game

The training half is simple: every few seconds NeuroDex tells you, through sound or a visual cue, whether your ratio is drifting toward the fast, non-dysrhythmic side or the slow, theta-heavy side. Over repeated sessions that feedback is what lets the brain practice nudging its own rhythm — the same principle behind clinical neurofeedback therapy, just aimed at this specific ratio instead of generic relaxation. Sitting still and watching a number for five minutes twice a day is a hard habit to keep, so we deliver that loop as a lightweight collection game — you earn Ions for time spent on the target side of the ratio, and spend them on a 51-specimen neuroscience collection. The game is just the delivery mechanism; the number underneath it is the actual project.

08 — Hardware

Built for NeuroSky

NeuroDex is tuned end to end for the NeuroSky ThinkGear chipset: MindWave, MindWave Mobile and MindWave Mobile 2. We read the raw 512 Hz wave rather than trusting the on-chip attention number, but we use the chip's own contact quality and blink detection to throw out the samples that would otherwise poison your score.

03 — Models

Artifact filters

A single dry forehead electrode picks up far more than thalamocortical rhythm: a blink swings the signal harder than any real EEG activity, a jaw clench buries the whole spectrum under muscle noise, and a loose headband reports pure junk. Any of that leaking into the theta or gamma bins would fake the exact signature this project is trying to measure, so every filter below runs live on the window before it ever reaches the Focus Index or the phenotyping ratio. Toggle one off and watch the reject count change.

These six run in the browser, in real time, on a budget of milliseconds — they're fast heuristics, not a diagnostic pipeline. For a slower, independent check, exported sessions can also be run back through an MNE-Python artifact pass offline (see the Progress tab), which flags muscle and amplitude artifacts using the same published methods researchers use on clinical EEG, so you can see how often the live filters agree with it.

Pilot 001 · Design stage

Thalamocortical dysrhythmia: a personal phenotyping pilot

n = 1, not diagnostic

Chronic tinnitus, chronic pain and depression are treated as unrelated conditions, but one theory says a subset of each shares a mechanism: thalamic circuits that should idle in a fast rhythm get stuck oscillating in theta instead, and that slow rhythm leaks into whichever cortical region the affected thalamic loop feeds. The symptom just depends on the target — auditory cortex gives tinnitus, somatosensory cortex gives pain, prefrontal and limbic circuits give depression. This pilot asks a narrower, answerable question: does a consumer EEG headset pick up that signature at all, and does training against it move the number.

The theory, briefly

Rodolfo Llínas proposed thalamocortical dysrhythmia in 1999: certain thalamic nuclei normally fire in a fast rhythm and switch to slow theta-range bursting under conditions like deafferentation or disrupted input. That theta bleeds into the cortical target the nucleus projects to, and surrounding cortex reacts with abnormally high-frequency activity at the edges of the affected zone — a halo of gamma around a core of theta. The theory is still debated; it is not settled neuroscience, and this pilot treats it as a hypothesis worth checking against real data, not a diagnosis to apply to anyone.

The two metrics

Spectral edge frequency (SEF95) Frequency below which 95% of spectral power sits
Low/high power ratio (theta + delta) ÷ (alpha + beta + gamma)

A depressed SEF95 and an elevated low/high ratio, held at rest over many baseline sessions, is the signature the theory predicts. Neither number is exotic — SEF95 is standard in anaesthesia depth monitoring, and the ratio is a simple reweighting of the same four band powers the app already computes four times a second.

Where it sits in the existing pipeline

No new signal-acquisition path is needed. The FFT and artifact filters already built for the Focus Index run on the same window, so both metrics are a second read of output that already exists: SEF95 from the cumulative power curve across the same frequency bins, the ratio from the same four band powers. The session log on Progress already stores a mean per session — phenotyping is a question of trend across many of those, not a new logging system.

The intervention

Closed-loop neurofeedback that rewards higher-frequency activity and suppresses the intruding theta is, mechanically, the training loop already running on the Session screen. The Focus Index is one way to read the same band powers; a TBR-based score that explicitly rewards (alpha+beta+gamma) over theta is another read of identical data. The pilot's second question is whether repeated sessions measurably shift that ratio over time, the same way any other trained signal might drift with practice.

What this does not show. One EEG channel at the forehead cannot localize activity to a specific cortical region the way the original theory's imaging studies could — there is no way to separate "auditory cortex" from "somatosensory cortex" with a single dry electrode. This pilot can only ask whether a resting frontal signature exists and whether it moves with training, not which downstream symptom it maps to. Treat every result here as exploratory self-experimentation.

Protocol

01

Baseline phenotype

10–15 resting sessions, no feedback shown, to get a stable SEF95 and low/high ratio and see how much they vary day to day before any training starts.

02

Compare to the model

Check whether the resting numbers actually sit where the dysrhythmia model predicts, or just reflect normal individual variation. Most people will not show a clean signature.

03

Closed-loop training

N sessions of feedback tuned to reward the high-frequency side of the ratio, using the same session structure as normal training, just rescored.

04

Re-phenotype

Repeat the resting measurement and check whether the ratio moved outside day-to-day noise, and whether it holds a week after training stops.
